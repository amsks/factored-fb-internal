\section{Setting}
\label{sec:prelim}

The successor measure of a policy $\pi$ is its discounted future-state occupancy,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sm}
M^\pi(s,a,X) = \E_\pi\!\left[ \sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t \ind{s_{t+1}\in X} \;\middle|\; s_0=s,a_0=a \right],
\qquad
Q_r^\pi(s,a) = \int r(x)\,dM^\pi(s,a,x),
\end{equation}
so one learned measure evaluates any downstream reward \citep{dayan1993improving,blier2021learning}.
Forward-backward (FB) representations \citep{touati2021learning,touati2023does} factorize its density against the dataset marginal $\rho$, $m_z(s,a,x) = F(s,a,z)^\top B(x)$, embed a reward as $z_r \propto \E_{x\sim\rho}[r(x)B(x)]$, and read values as $Q_r \approx F(s,a,z_r)^\top z_r$.
The standard estimator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fb-loss}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FB}} = \tfrac12 \E_{x\sim\rho} \left[ (m_z-\gamma\bar m_z)^2 \right] - \E \left[ m_z(s,a,s') - \gamma\bar m_z(s,a,s') \right] + \lambda_{\mathrm{ortho}} \mathcal L_{\mathrm{ortho}}:
\end{equation}
random queries get a squared residual; the observed successor gets a linear positive term.

\section{Why stationary segments dominate the FB objective}
\label{sec:play}

Manipulation play alternates brief object-changing interactions with long periods at persistent configurations: approach, grasp, transport, release, then rest.
On \texttt{cube-single-play-v0} the cube is stationary on $48\%$ of transitions and stationary runs have median length $44$ steps; after entering a $4$\,cm region a trajectory accumulates $25$--$31$ discounted visits there.
On \texttt{cube-double} each cube is stationary on $72\%$ of transitions; on \texttt{scene}, drawer and window on $91\%$ and buttons on $99.6\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:playdata}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/playdata.png}
\caption{\emph{Left:} three \texttt{cube-single} play trajectories. \emph{Right:} discounted occupancy accumulated after first entering a $4$\,cm goal region. Most successor mass accumulates after arrival.}
\label{fig:playdata}
\end{figure}

This structure repeatedly reinforces the same successor.
A rest of length $L$ contributes $L$ near-identical positive terms, one per transition. The transition that brought the cube there appears once. A $44$-step rest therefore gives the same configuration roughly forty opportunities to influence the objective.
Repeated stationary transitions create two problems.
First, the linear positive term in Equation~\ref{eq:fb-loss} can increase the scale of the critic without bound.
Second, when the successor target is a region indicator $h(s',g)=\ind{s'\in B_\epsilon(g)}$, the Bellman objective admits an action-independent constant solution $c^\star = \E_\rho[h]/(1-\gamma)$.
In either case, the objective can be reduced without learning which actions move the object.

A frozen-representation experiment separates the objective from representation capacity (Figure~\ref{fig:divergence}).
With a backward representation frozen from a checkpoint supporting $0.97$ task success, the standard FB objective grows the critic scale from $154$ to $5.4\times10^4$ within $65$k steps and success falls to $0.42$; orthonormalization bounds the scale and caps success at $0.225$.
A capable representation therefore does not rescue the objective. The instability follows the estimator.

\section{\DensityFB: soft and hard variants}
\label{sec:method}

We consider a goal-conditioned policy $\pi(a\mid s,g)$ and write $M(\cdot\mid s,a,g)$ for its successor measure, restricted to the goal-relevant object coordinates $x=\phi_{\mathrm{obj}}(s)$ (the pushforward of the state successor measure, which is exact for any reward defined on object coordinates).
Since the total discounted mass is $1/(1-\gamma)$ for every $(s,a)$, multiplying by $1-\gamma$ yields a probability distribution:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nu}
\nu(\cdot\mid s,a,g) = (1-\gamma)\, M(\cdot\mid s,a,g).
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Role of the goal.}
Goals are not required by \DensityFB{}; they index the policies whose successor measures we learn.
For a fixed policy, $g$ could simply be removed.
Hindsight relabeling of the play data gives us a family of goal-conditioned policies $\pi_g$, and we learn the corresponding family of measures $M^{\pi_g}$.
This is analogous to standard FB, where a latent variable $z$ indexes a family of policies and measures; training uses no rewards or task labels in either case.

The key change relative to FB's estimator is that $F^\top B$ becomes a score. A softmax over sampled query configurations turns the scores into a normalized distribution,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:density-softmax}
f(s,a,g,u) = F(s,a,g)^\top B(\tilde u)/\tau,
\qquad
w_j(s,a,g) = \frac{\exp f(s,a,g,u_j)}{\sum_k \exp f(s,a,g,u_k)},
\end{equation}
over a query set $U=\{u_j\}$ redrawn from the dataset marginal at every update (the batch's own next object configurations).
Non-negativity and unit mass hold by construction, and the query count is a Monte-Carlo sample size, not a discretization: the checkpoint stores only $F$ and $B$.

Each update predicts how one unit of normalized future occupancy is distributed across candidate object configurations.
The target assigns $1-\gamma$ mass to the observed successor and bootstraps the remaining $\gamma$ from the next state, and is fit by cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:density-loss}
t = \sg \left[ (1-\gamma)\, \kappa(\cdot\mid x') + \gamma\, \bar w(s',a^+,g) \right],
\qquad
\mathcal L = - \E \Big[ \sum_j t_j \log w_j \Big],
\qquad a^+ \sim \pi_{\bar\omega}(\cdot\mid s',g),
\end{equation}
where $\bar w$ is the target-network prediction and $\pi_{\bar\omega}$ is the target copy of the goal-conditioned actor introduced below.
The first term places $1-\gamma$ of the probability mass around the observed next configuration; the remaining $\gamma$ is inherited from the next state's predicted occupancy.
Both terms lie on the simplex, so $t$ is itself a distribution.
We evaluate two immediate targets, differing only in how the immediate mass is assigned:
\begin{align}
\text{soft:}\quad &\kappa_\epsilon(u_j\mid x') \propto \exp\!\left( -\tfrac12 \|(u_j-x')/\epsilon\|^2 \right),
\label{eq:kappa-soft}\\
\text{hard:}\quad &\kappa_j(x'_i) = \ind{j = i},
\label{eq:kappa-hard}
\end{align}
with $\epsilon$ the task tolerance.
The hard target is an index-level one-hot: $U$ is built from the batch's next configurations, so row $i$'s successor occupies slot $i$.
This keeps the target on the simplex even when several rows share the same object coordinates, which stationary segments make common; a coordinate-equality indicator would place mass greater than one on duplicates.

This removes the scale inflation without removing dwell occupancy.
A long rest can still place high probability on that configuration, but the total predicted mass remains one.
The model can therefore represent that the cube tends to stay where it was left without increasing critic scale simply because the same configuration appears many times.
Hard and soft targets differ only in how they assign the immediate $1-\gamma$ mass: exactly to the observed successor, or across its $\epsilon$-neighborhood.

\paragraph{Fixed point and contraction.}
Normalization preserves the successor-measure fixed point: for the hard variant it only rescales the measure by $1-\gamma$, constraining total mass; the soft variant additionally smooths the immediate successor over an $\epsilon$-neighborhood, so its fixed point is the correspondingly smoothed measure.
Writing $\mathcal T\nu \,(\cdot\mid s,a) = (1-\gamma)\,\E[\kappa(\cdot\mid x_1)] + \gamma\,\E_{s_1,a^+}\left[ \nu(\cdot\mid s_1,a^+) \right]$ for the operator whose sample version is Equation~\ref{eq:density-loss}, for any two conditional measures
\begin{equation}
\sup_{s,a}\, \big\| \mathcal T\nu_1 - \mathcal T\nu_2 \big\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\;\le\; \gamma\, \sup_{s,a}\, \big\| \nu_1 - \nu_2 \big\|_{\mathrm{TV}},
\end{equation}
since the immediate term cancels and the expectation is an average.
The operator is therefore a $\gamma$-contraction in total variation.
For the hard target, normalization changes the scale and fitting objective but leaves the underlying successor-measure fixed point unchanged.
The soft target additionally replaces each successor with an $\epsilon$-smoothed distribution.
In either case, critic scale is no longer a free parameter.

For control, the actor maximizes the predicted mass near its goal, $q(s,a,g) = w(s,a,g)^\top \kappa(\cdot\mid g)$, computed at frozen measure parameters, inside the standard flow behavior-cloning objective:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:actor}
\mathcal L_\pi = \lambda_{\mathrm{BC}}\, \mathcal L_{\mathrm{BC\text{-}flow}} \;-\; \E\!\left[ \frac{ q(s, \pi(s,g), g) }{ s_q } \right],
\qquad s_q = \E\big[\,\big|q - \E[q]\big|\,\big],\ \lambda_{\mathrm{BC}} = 3,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{BC\text{-}flow}}$ is the flow-matching behavior-cloning loss and $s_q$ normalizes the magnitude of the critic term.
The predicted occupancy contains a large action-independent component. The actor only needs differences between actions, so we center $q$ before computing its magnitude normalizer.
The behavior-cloning row in Table~\ref{tab:main} trains the same actor with the $q$ term removed.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:densityfb} summarizes one gradient step.
Black lines are shared with standard FB; {\color{fbdiff}blue} lines are where \DensityFB{} differs; the immediate target is the single line where the two variants differ ({\color{softvar}soft}, {\color{hardvar}hard}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\DensityFB{}, one gradient step. Black: shared with standard FB (which draws task vectors $z$ where we draw goals $g$). {\color{fbdiff}Blue}: changed relative to FB. {\color{softvar}Orange}/{\color{hardvar}teal}: the soft/hard fork, one line.}
\label{alg:densityfb}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Sample transitions $\{(s_i,a_i,s'_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and goals $\{g_i\}$ from $\mathcal D$
\State {\color{fbdiff} Build the query set from the batch's own successors: $u_j = \phi_{\mathrm{obj}}(s'_j)$ for $j=1,\dots,n$}
\State Compute scores $f_{ij} = F(s_i,a_i,g_i)^\top B(\tilde u_j)/\tau$
\State {\color{fbdiff} Normalize the scores into distributions: $w_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax}_j(f_{ij})$ \Comment{FB reads $f$ as an unconstrained density}}
\State {\color{softvar} soft: $\kappa_{ij} \propto \exp\!\big({-\tfrac12}\|(u_j-x'_i)/\epsilon\|^2\big)$} \textbf{~or~} {\color{hardvar} hard: $\kappa_{ij} = \ind{j=i}$}
\State Sample bootstrap actions $a^+_i \sim \pi_{\bar\omega}(\cdot\mid s'_i,g_i)$
\State {\color{fbdiff} Form simplex targets $t_i = \sg\!\left[(1-\gamma)\,\kappa_i + \gamma\,\bar w(s'_i,a^+_i,g_i)\right]$}
\State {\color{fbdiff} Update $F,B$ with cross-entropy $-\tfrac1n\sum_i\sum_j t_{ij}\log w_{ij}$ \Comment{FB: squared loss, Eq.~\ref{eq:fb-loss}}}
\State {\color{fbdiff} Update the actor by Eq.~\ref{eq:actor} with $q_i = w(s_i,\pi(s_i,g_i),g_i)^\top \kappa(\cdot\mid g_i)$ at frozen $F,B$}
\State Soft-update the target networks $\bar F,\bar B,\pi_{\bar\omega}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Goal-conditioned results}
\label{sec:main-results}

OGBench state-based manipulation and locomotion \citep{park2024ogbench}, $500$k gradient steps, no reward labels during training, native evaluation (five tasks, $50$ episodes each), three seeds, mean $\pm$ $95\%$ confidence interval ($t$-based).
We evaluate each representation with two actor extractors, flow behavior cloning and Gaussian DDPG+BC, to separate representation quality from policy extraction.
Behavior cloning trains the same actor with the $q$ term removed and learns no successor representation: $0.111$ on \texttt{cube-single}, $0.019$ on \texttt{cube-double}, $0.071$ on \texttt{scene} ($0.064/0.080/0.068$, three seeds).

On \texttt{cube-single}, both \DensityFB{} variants reach about $0.92$, leaving little room to distinguish them.
The harder manipulation tasks separate the methods more clearly.
On \texttt{cube-double}, soft \DensityFB{} reaches $0.736$ with the flow actor, compared with $0.000$ for plain FB and $0.019$ for behavior cloning; with DDPG+BC it reaches $0.640$, against a published $0.40$ for goal-conditioned IQL at twice the training budget.
On \texttt{scene}, \DensityFB{} reaches about $0.64$ while plain FB remains near zero.
The \texttt{scene} average also hides a consistent task split: all seeds solve \texttt{open}, \texttt{unlock-and-lock} and \texttt{rearrange-medium} ($0.88$--$1.0$). \texttt{put-in-drawer} ($0.02$--$0.50$) and \texttt{rearrange-hard} ($0.0$--$0.04$) remain difficult.

\paragraph{Manipulation results are similar under DDPG+BC.}
Switching from flow behavior cloning to DDPG+BC lowers soft \DensityFB{} from $0.929$ to $0.879$ on \texttt{cube-single} and from $0.736$ to $0.640$ on \texttt{cube-double}. The improvement is not specific to the flow actor.
On \texttt{antmaze-medium} the same switch moves \DensityFB{} from $0.563$ to $0.829$, so actor extraction matters much more there.

\paragraph{\DensityFB{} does not improve AntMaze.}
\texttt{antmaze-medium} removes the structure that motivated \DensityFB{}: the goal-relevant variable is the agent itself, so there is no manipulated object that remains stationary while actions continue.
Consistent with this, \DensityFB{} does not improve over our matched goal-conditioned IQL control under DDPG+BC ($0.829$ versus $0.837$), and both sit below the strongest published method on that benchmark ($0.96$ for HIQL).
This supports the narrower hypothesis that \DensityFB{} helps when play data contains long stationary segments in the goal-relevant object variables.

\section{Reward evaluation}
\label{sec:reward-transfer}

Goal reaching only tests whether the learned measure supports the benchmark goals.
Reward transfer asks whether the same measure can evaluate a reward that was never used for training.
At test time we approximate the integral of $r$ against the learned successor distribution with a pool $U_P$ of $16{,}384$ object configurations drawn from the dataset: we evaluate $r$ on the pool once and take its expectation under the predicted occupancy $w$, which gives $w^\top r(U_P)/(1-\gamma)$.
Because stationary transitions concentrate predicted occupancy near the current object configuration, finite-pool smoothing matters most there.
We therefore correct the reward estimate at $x_0$ explicitly.
Writing $\kappa(\cdot\mid x_0)$ for the kernel weights of $x_0$ over the pool, we replace the smoothed estimate of the reward at that configuration, $\kappa(\cdot\mid x_0)^\top r(U_P)$, with its exact value $r(x_0)$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:density-readout}
\widehat Q_r(s,a) = \frac{ r(x_0) + \left( w-\kappa(\cdot\mid x_0) \right)^\top r(U_P) }{ 1-\gamma }.
\end{equation}
The correction is action independent. It changes calibration and leaves action ranking unchanged.
The resulting readout is linear in $r$ and requires no retraining.
To act, we score candidate goals under the new reward, choose the highest-valued one from the initial state, and execute its goal-conditioned policy.
This replaces FB's analytic reward-to-$z$ mapping with search over the learned goal-conditioned policy family.

\paragraph{Reward construction.}
We test three reward families, all defined only from object coordinates, valued in $[0,1]$, and never observed during training (Figure~\ref{fig:rewardfams}; internal names \texttt{dirac}/\texttt{dense}/\texttt{region}):
\emph{sparse}, the benchmark's tolerance indicator $\ind{\|x-g\|\le\epsilon}$ (conjunctive over object blocks) at the five task goals;
\emph{dense}, the same centers with distance-based shaping $\exp(-d(x,g)/2\epsilon)$ in tolerance-scaled coordinates;
and \emph{held-out regions}, the same sparse indicator centered at reachable dataset configurations that are not benchmark goals (drawn by hindsight from within episodes, kept only with at least five relabeled samples inside the region).
Dense versus sparse tests sensitivity to reward shaping while keeping the goals fixed; sparse versus held-out regions keeps the reward form fixed while changing where in object space the reward sits.
$25$ episodes per task, three seeds.
The fixed-partition row is the same objective with the continuous query set replaced by $256$ fixed prototypes, the discretized ablation.

The largest difference appears on rewards centered away from the benchmark goals: on \texttt{cube-single}, held-out-region transfer rises from $0.133$ with the fixed partition to $0.293$ with soft \DensityFB{} and $0.647$ with the hard variant (Table~\ref{tab:partition}).

\texttt{cube-double} is also where the published methods are closest to each other and to zero: six of the eight fall below $5$.

Reward transfer remains harder than direct goal reaching.
On \texttt{cube-double}, the same soft checkpoint reaches $0.736$ under ordinary goal conditioning and $0.549$ when the same goal is specified through the sparse reward readout.
Because the checkpoint is identical, this gap is introduced by the reward-readout and goal-selection pipeline rather than by training the representation itself.
An oracle that selects the reward center directly would separate readout error from goal-selection error.
