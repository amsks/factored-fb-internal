The gap decomposes as
\begin{equation}
    -19.8
    =
    +0.2_{\text{approach}}
    +2.4_{\text{grasp}}
    -14.6_{\text{maintain}}
    -7.8_{\text{transport}}.
\end{equation}
This is the main empirical finding. \fb{} does not primarily fail by missing the cube. It fails after contact should have made the object controllable.

% Transport failures are not simply small errors near the target.
A near-miss analysis supports this interpretation. Across the \fb{} and \gciql{} state rollouts, there are no episodes in which the agent finishes while holding the cube within one $5$ cm success radius of the goal. The transport failures are therefore not fine placement errors near the target. They are failures to bring the held object into the target neighborhood.

\subsection{Dataset support does not explain the pattern}

% Failed transport states are not obviously much farther from the offline data.
The support diagnostic does not indicate a simple out-of-distribution explanation. If transport failures were mainly caused by leaving the dataset manifold, failed transport states should have much larger nearest-neighbor distances than successful transport states. The differences in Table~\ref{tab:state_coverage} are small.

\begin{table}[htb]

\midrule

This result does not show that support is irrelevant. Nearest-neighbor distance is a coarse statistic, and local action support may still differ between successful and failed rollouts. However, the observed transport failures do not appear to arise simply because failed states are far from the offline dataset.

\subsection{The \fb{} representation gives weak terminal guidance}

% FB value does not monotonically guide the cube toward the goal.

For each episode, we compute the Spearman correlation between the extracted value $V(s)=Q_z(s,\pi(s))$ and negative cube-to-goal distance $-d(s,g)$. A positive correlation means that value increases as the cube gets closer to the goal. The correlations in Table~\ref{tab:state_value_gradient} are weak for both successful and transport-failed episodes.

Together, these diagnostics support the contact-bottleneck interpretation. The main issue is not just reaching the cube or entering contact. The representation and policy must distinguish states in which the cube is geometrically close, physically controlled, and stably transportable from states in which only some of these conditions hold.

\section{Extending the Protocol Across URL Methods}
\label{sec:extensions}

% The evaluation labels are method-agnostic.
The state-only decomposition is not tied to \fb{} or \gciql{}. The same labels can be applied to \crl{}, \psm{}, and model-based methods without changing the evaluation protocol. The rows below separate completed measurements from planned diagnostics.

\begin{table}[htb]
\centering
\caption{State-only cross-method diagnostic table. Values are the outcome distribution as a percentage of episodes (mean across $10$ seeds), and each row sums to $100\%$. The \fb{}, \gciql{}, \crl{}, and \rldp{} rows are completed measurements; \psm{} and \dreamer{} rows indicate planned diagnostics rather than completed empirical results.}
\label{tab:cross_method_template}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule

The completed rows show that the post-contact bottleneck is not specific to \fb{}. \rldp{} secures the cube most reliably of any method, with the lowest grasp-failure rate ($9.6\%$), yet it has the highest maintain-grasp failure ($44.0\%$): nearly half of its episodes lift the cube and then drop it. Its failure profile is thus an amplified version of the \fb{} pattern, with the deficit concentrated even more sharply after pick-up. \crl{} fails more uniformly, splitting its failures across grasp ($23.8\%$), maintain ($28.6\%$), and transport ($24.0\%$). In all three lower-scoring methods the success gap relative to \gciql{} is dominated by maintain-grasp and transport outcomes rather than by approach, reinforcing that the shared difficulty is preserving and exploiting contact, not acquiring it.

For \crl{}, the key test is whether contrastive state-goal representations mis-rank phase-relevant states. Future-random separation should be computed separately for pre-grasp, lifted, dropped, and transport states, not only over all states. For \psm{}, the key test is basis coverage: whether any proto-policy or proto-basis assigns substantial successor mass to stable grasped transport. For a model-based method, the key test is phase-transition prediction: whether the learned model predicts contact acquisition, lift, slip, and drop transitions accurately enough to support imagined control.
