\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Our experiments support one chain: state-based URL methods do not mainly fail to reach the cube; they fail after acquiring control, and the learned scores do not reliably distinguish observation-near states with different post-contact futures. 
We present this in three steps: where policies fail, whether dataset support explains it, and whether the learned representation tells success-bound from fail-bound grasps apart.
Per-task success, exact outcome counts, and the full diagnostic tables are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.

\subsection{Scalar success obscures policy failure}

Aggregate state-based success ranks the methods but says nothing about where they fail. 
Across $10$ evaluation seeds, the overall Interquartile Mean (IQM)~\citep{agarwal-neurips21a} success rates are \gciql{} ($68.3 \pm 3.7$), \fb{} ($50.0 \pm 7.0$), \crl{} ($25.9 \pm 2.0$), and \rldp{} ($22.7 \pm 6.0$).\protect\footnotemark{}
Per-task results are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.
\footnotetext{Aggregate success uses the stochastic evaluation policy (IQM over seeds); the phase decomposition uses deterministic closed-loop rollouts, so per-method success differs slightly. We report each metric consistently within its own analysis.}

The phase decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:method}) localizes the failure (Figures~\ref{fig:funnel} and \ref{fig:outcomes}). 
Every method reaches the cube and most acquire a grasp, but the survival curve collapses \emph{after} control acquisition (Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}), and the terminal-outcome mass sits in maintain-grasp and transport rather than approach (Figure~\ref{fig:outcomes}). 
Relative to \gciql{}, \fb{}'s $19.8\text{ pp}$ deficit decomposes as $+0.2$ approach, $+2.4$ grasp, $-14.6$ maintain, $-7.8$ transport: almost entirely post-contact. 
The pattern is not specific to \fb{}; \rldp{} acquires control reliably yet ends without maintained control in $44\%$ of episodes, and across the lower-scoring methods the post-contact share of failures is $69$-$87\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). 
Because the phase labels read no value, representation, or model, the protocol extends unchanged to other methods.

\subsection{Post-contact failures are not explained by support alone}

The next diagnostics group individual states. 
Because both the play data and the policy rollouts cycle through pick-and-place many times per trajectory, we pool states into three instantaneous regimes defined by gripper and lift state: \emph{approach} (pre-contact), \emph{pickup} (gripper closed, cube on the table), and \emph{carry} (cube lifted and held). 
These regimes are distinct from the terminal failure phases: a maintain-grasp failure and a transport failure differ only by how the episode ends, and both originate within the carry regime.

A natural confound is that post-contact failures are simply out-of-distribution. 
Figure~\ref{fig:support} reports the failure-minus-success nearest-neighbor distance from rollout states to the offline data, by regime and post-contact failure mode. 
Failed states are often farther from the data in pickup and carry regimes, especially for maintain-control failures (e.g.\ \crl{} pickup, $+1.42$). 
Dataset support therefore contributes, but the deltas do not track the method success ordering.
But the same elevation appears for \gciql{}, which mostly succeeds, and the deltas do not track the method success ordering, so support is a contributing factor, not the mechanism. 
The full per-regime, per-outcome distances are in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.

\subsection{Learned representations alias success- and fail-bound grasps}

If support does not explain the failure, what is the learned representation missing?
We test whether \emph{any} representation---raw state, learned state embedding, or learned successor map---separates success-bound from fail-bound in-hand grasps, and find that none does.

First, geometry can be present in the representation without yielding control.
For the methods with an explicit backward embedding, \rldp{}'s $B$ resolves cube-to-goal distance far better than \fb{}'s (nonlinear $R^2$ $0.75$ vs $0.21$), yet \rldp{} has the worse maintain-grasp failure (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).
Resolving geometry is not the bottleneck.

The decisive test is a representation ladder (Figure~\ref{fig:repsep_ladder}).
On in-hand (grasp/carry) training states we fit a linear probe to predict success-bound versus fail-bound \emph{per task} (fixed goal) and average across the five tasks.
Three findings.
(i) The instantaneous configuration barely determines the outcome at all: even the \emph{raw} full state caps at AUC $0.64$, and the gripper alone (opening and contact) is at chance ($0.50$)---how the cube is held at that instant says almost nothing about whether the grasp will hold.
(ii) The learned state embedding $\phi(s)$ matches but never exceeds this raw ceiling (\fb{} $0.64$, \rldp{} $0.64$).
(iii) Most tellingly, separability \emph{decays} as we move toward what drives behavior: the forward map $F(s,\pi,z)$---trained to predict successor occupancy---does no better than $\phi$ (\fb{} $0.59$, \rldp{} $0.61$), and the scalar value $Q=F(s,\pi,z)\cdot z$ that the policy actually follows collapses to chance (\fb{} $0.51$, \rldp{} $0.54$; Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:value_aliasing}).
The representation built to encode the future does not separate futures any better than the static pose, and the value the policy maximizes does not separate them at all.
Probing the policy's own rollouts tells the same story but is high-variance over the ${\sim}50$ evaluation episodes (per-method AUC swings by ${\pm}0.2$ across rollout draws), so we rely on the stable per-task training-state probe; the per-method representation scatters are in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.

The value-space view agrees (Figure~\ref{fig:aliasing}).
For each fail-bound state we find its nearest success-bound state in observation space and ask whether the value ranks the success-bound state higher; a value that separated controllable from about-to-fail states would score near $1$.
All methods stay only weakly above chance ($0.48$--$0.60$, with small across-seed spread).
Even for observation-near pairs with opposite futures, the learned scores barely separate them.
This is the empirical signature predicted by the interaction-gap view: the missing object is not coarse cube geometry, but post-contact controllability, which is absent from the instantaneous observation and is not recovered by the successor map.

\paragraph{Takeaway.}
The phase diagnostic changes the interpretation of state-based URL failures in cube manipulation.
The evaluated methods usually reach the object and often acquire control, but many failures occur after acquisition, when the policy must preserve control and transport the cube.
Support gaps in pickup and carry regimes contribute to this failure mode, but they do not track the method ordering.
The learned scores also weakly rank observation-near states with different post-contact futures.
Together, these results suggest that post-contact controllability is a useful target for future offline URL objectives in manipulation.
