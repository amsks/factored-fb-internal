\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Our experiments test the interaction-state hypothesis from Section~\ref{sec:theory}.
The evaluated methods rarely fail during approach; most performance differences arise after control has been acquired.
We then ask whether this pattern is explained by state-space support and whether learned values distinguish acquired-control states with different task outcomes.
These diagnostics identify where policies fail and whether learned values preserve the interaction-state distinctions needed for control, but they are not designed to isolate a single architectural cause.
Per-goal success, exact counts, and the full diagnostic tables are in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.

\subsection{Aggregate success obscures where policies fail}

Aggregate state-based success ranks the methods but says nothing about where they fail. 
Across $10$ evaluation seeds, the overall inter-quartile mean (IQM)~\citep{agarwal-neurips21a} success rates are \gciql{} ($71.8 \pm 4.1$), \fb{} ($52.0 \pm 5.5$), \psm{} ($36.7 \pm 4.5$), \crl{} ($25.8 \pm 2.3$), and \rldp{} ($22.7 \pm 6.0$).\protect\footnotemark{}
\footnotetext{Aggregate success uses the environment's success signal under stochastic evaluation. The phase analysis uses deterministic rollouts and a separate diagnostic definition of success that additionally requires final retention and uses a $5$ cm cube-to-goal position threshold (Table~\ref{tab:failure_criteria}); its success percentage is therefore not expected to match aggregate benchmark success exactly.}

The phase decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:method} localizes where the methods fail (Figures~\ref{fig:funnel} and \ref{fig:outcomes}).
Every method almost always completes the approach phase, and most rollouts satisfy the control-acquisition criterion.
The largest differences arise afterward, in maintain-control and transport failures.

\fb{} and \gciql{} differ little during approach and control acquisition.
Relative to \gciql{}, \fb{} has $17.1$ percentage points more maintain-control failures and $10.3$ points more transport failures, partly offset by $3.2$ points fewer control-acquisition failures.
The same pattern appears for other methods: \psm{} and \rldp{} acquire control but fail to maintain it in $36\%$ and $44\%$ of episodes, respectively.
Across the lower-performing methods, $65$--$83\%$ of failed episodes occur after the control-acquisition criterion has been satisfied (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).
Because the phase labels are computed only from simulator states, the same analysis can be applied to other methods without access to their learned values, representations, or models.

\subsection{State-support gaps covary with performance but do not explain value ranking}

The support analysis groups individual states according to gripper and cube state.
Because both the play data and policy rollouts contain repeated pick-and-place attempts, we use three state categories: \emph{approach} (pre-contact), \emph{pickup} (gripper closed, cube on the table), and \emph{carry} (cube lifted and held).
These categories are distinct from the terminal failure phases: maintain-control and transport failures are assigned according to how the episode ends, whereas approach, pickup, and carry describe states encountered during the rollout.

One possible explanation is that failures after control acquisition occur in regions that are poorly represented in the offline data.
Figure~\ref{fig:support} reports the difference in nearest-neighbor distance to the offline dataset between failed and successful rollout states, grouped by state category and failure phase.
Failed states are often farther from the data in the pickup and carry categories, especially for maintain-control failures (e.g.\ \crl{} pickup, $+1.83$).

These state-support gaps broadly covary with performance.
\gciql{}, the strongest method, shows almost no gap in the plotted pickup and carry categories, whereas every lower-performing method shows positive gaps.
However, the gaps do not reproduce the exact performance ordering among the lower-performing methods, so state support is at most a partial explanation.
The value-ranking probe below begins from in-hand states sampled directly from the offline dataset, so the initial states are supported by construction.
Nevertheless, \rldp{} and \psm{} still rank eventual task success near chance, and \fb{} only weakly.
This shows that initial-state support alone does not explain the ranking failures, but it does not rule out limited action or transition support during the subsequent rollout.
Weak value ranking may drive a policy toward poorly supported states, limited support may degrade the learned value or policy, or both effects may occur.
Our diagnostics do not determine the direction of causality.

\subsection{Learned values differ in how well they rank acquired-control states}

Because initial-state support alone does not account for the ranking failures observed on states drawn from the dataset, we next investigate whether each learned value ranks in-hand grasp states by eventual success under the method's own policy.
Coarse cube-to-goal geometry is not sufficient to explain performance.
For methods with an explicit backward embedding, the embeddings learned by \psm{} and \rldp{} predict cube-to-goal distance substantially better than the embedding learned by \fb{} (nonlinear $R^2$ of $0.90$ and $0.75$ versus $0.21$).
Nevertheless, both methods have higher maintain-control failure rates than \fb{} (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).
Encoding cube-to-goal geometry therefore does not guarantee that the policy will maintain control.


We evaluate value quality through simulator resets (Figure~\ref{fig:cf_value}).
We reset the simulator to $200$ in-hand grasp states drawn from the offline data and pair each with the five held-out goals, giving $1{,}000$ state--goal pairs per agent seed.
Because the flow policy is stochastic, we roll out $K{=}10$ independent continuations from each pair and label the pair a success when at least half of its continuations reach the goal.
Each pair is evaluated independently, and the probe covers states from the offline dataset rather than only states generated by the learned policy.
We then plot success rate as a function of the learned value percentile.
We summarize ranking quality with the rank-AUC, i.e.\ the ROC-AUC of the learned value against this binary success label---the probability that a successful grasp receives a higher value than an unsuccessful one---computed within each goal over its $200$ grasps and averaged across the five goals and the agent's training seeds (we use ``rank-AUC'' for this quantity throughout).
Because the success labels are generated separately under each method's own policy, this probe measures whether a method's value is consistent with its own downstream outcomes rather than comparing all methods against a single shared target.
\gciql{}'s value is the most predictive (rank-AUC $0.82$): its top-value grasps succeed ${\approx}99\%$ of the time, its bottom-value grasps ${\approx}54\%$.
For \rldp{} and \psm{}, the curves are nearly flat (rank-AUC $0.51$ and $0.52$), indicating that higher values do not correspond to higher success rates.
\fb{} shows a weaker relationship (rank-AUC approximately $0.64$), while \crl{} is intermediate ($0.73$).
Thus \rldp{} and \psm{} rank outcomes near chance, \fb{} provides a weaker but positive ranking, and \crl{} is intermediate between \fb{} and \gciql{}; only \gciql{} ranks acquired-control states reliably.

We next ask whether the weak ranking arises in the learned representation or in the scalar value derived from it.
Using each agent's simulator-reset success labels, we fit a linear readout from the learned representation and compare its ranking with that of the learned value (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}, Figure~\ref{fig:cf_ladder}).
The probe suggests different bottlenecks for \fb{}, \rldp{}, and \psm{}.
\fb{} is consistent with a readout bottleneck: a linear readout of its forward map reaches rank-AUC 0.83, while its learned scalar value reaches only 0.64.
\rldp{} is consistent with a representation bottleneck under this probe: its linear representation readout reaches $0.68$, below the raw-state baseline of $0.72$, while its learned value reaches $0.51$.
We treat ``representation bottleneck'' cautiously, since a lower linear-probe score does not establish that the relevant information is absent---it may be nonlinearly encoded.
\psm{} shows the same pattern more sharply: although its backward embedding resolves cube-to-goal geometry best of any method (nonlinear $R^2$ $0.90$; Table~\ref{tab:geometry_control}), a linear readout of the representation its value uses does not exceed the raw-state baseline for counterfactual success ($0.66$ versus $0.73$), and the value itself ranks near chance ($0.52$). Resolving cube-to-goal geometry therefore does not imply that the representation encodes which grasps the policy will keep.
For \gciql{}, the learned value retains most of the linearly decodable signal available in its value features (readout $0.92$, value $0.82$), the strongest value ranking under this diagnostic.

These probes suggest that weak ranking can arise either in the learned representation or in the scalar readout, but they do not identify the readout form as the sole cause.
\gciql{} differs from \fb{}, \crl{}, \rldp{}, and \psm{} in objective, actor training, critic architecture, implicit regularization, and optimization stability, not only in its value readout.
The evidence therefore supports a narrower conclusion: \rldp{} and \psm{} rank acquired-control states near chance and \fb{} only weakly, whereas \crl{} and especially \gciql{} rank them more reliably under this diagnostic.
A matched readout ablation would be needed to attribute the difference specifically to bilinear versus MLP value form.

\paragraph{Takeaway.}
The phase decomposition changes the interpretation of state-based URL failures in cube manipulation.
The evaluated methods usually complete approach and acquire control, but many failures occur afterward, in the maintain-control and transport phases.
Failed pickup and carry states are farther from the offline dataset for the lower-performing methods but not for \gciql{}, so state-support gaps broadly covary with performance.
However, initial-state support alone does not explain why \rldp{} and \psm{} rank in-dataset grasp states near chance.
Among the lower-performing methods, \rldp{} and \psm{} rank acquired-control states near chance and \fb{} only weakly, while \crl{} is intermediate; only \gciql{} ranks them reliably.
Distinguishing such states is therefore a useful target for future offline URL objectives in manipulation.
