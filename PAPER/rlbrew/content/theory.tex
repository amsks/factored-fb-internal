\section{Interaction-conditioned successor gaps in manipulation}

\subsection{Manipulation as interaction-conditioned control}

Let $\tilde{s}\in\widetilde{\mathcal S}$ denote the full physical state and let $y=\Omega(\tilde{s})\in\mathcal Y$ denote the state observation available to the policy. 
In \texttt{OGBENCH cube-single}, $y$ contains robot proprioception, end-effector pose, gripper opening and contact indicators, and cube pose. 
However, it does not explicitly encode all physical variables that determine grasp stability, such as the full contact wrench, frictional margin, slip state, or other unobserved contact quantities.

We therefore distinguish the observed state $y$ from an interaction mode $\iota(\tilde{s})\in\mathcal I$, where $\mathcal I$ may include free motion, partial contact, stable grasp, slipping contact, and dropped contact. 
The interaction mode determines how actions couple robot motion to object motion. 
We write the full-state transition kernel as interaction-conditioned: $P(d\tilde{s}'\mid \tilde{s},a) = P_{\iota(\tilde{s})}(d\tilde{s}'\mid \Omega(\tilde{s}),a)$.

This formulation separates observed geometry from controllability. 
Before grasp, an end-effector motion may leave the cube fixed. 
During a stable grasp, a similar motion may transport the cube. 
During unstable contact, the same motion may cause the cube to slip or drop. 
Thus two states can be close in observed geometry while inducing different effective transition kernels because their interaction modes differ.

The key implication is that proximity in observation space need not imply proximity in successor-measure space. 
A stable grasp and a partial grasp may have nearly identical cube pose, gripper pose, and goal distance, but their long-horizon futures can differ sharply.

\subsection{Success before loss of control}

Let $G_g = \{\tilde{s}\in\widetilde{\mathcal S}:\|o(\tilde{s})-g\|\leq \epsilon_G\}$ be the task success set for goal $g$. 
Let $D\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal S}$ denote the instantaneous loss-of-control set, for example states in which the cube has been dropped or is no longer controllably held.

Define the hitting times $\tau_{G_g} = \inf\{t\geq 0:\tilde{s}_t\in G_g\}$ and $\tau_D = \inf\{t\geq 0:\tilde{s}_t\in D\}$.

The task-relevant long-horizon quantity is the reach-before-drop probability given by $u^\pi(\tilde{s},a;g) = \Pr^\pi(\tau_{G_g}<\tau_D \mid \tilde{s}_0=\tilde{s},a_0=a)$.

This quantity is not the full successor measure. 
Rather, it is a task-relevant projection of the successor measure: it asks whether future occupancy reaches the goal before entering a loss-of-control regime. 
Equivalently, it is the value function of a reach-avoid problem in which reaching $G_g$ before $D$ receives terminal reward $1$, while entering $D$ first receives terminal reward $0$.

A discounted variant can also be defined as $u^\pi_\gamma(\tilde{s},a;g) = \mathbb E^\pi [ \gamma^{\tau_{G_g}} \mathbf 1\{\tau_{G_g}<\tau_D\} \mid \tilde{s}_0=\tilde{s},a_0=a ]$, but the undiscounted probability is the quantity most directly aligned with the phase-decomposition diagnostic used below.

\subsection{Contact creates local successor gaps}

For an observation scale $\epsilon>0$, define the local interaction gap:
\begin{equation}
\Delta^\pi_\epsilon(g) = \sup_{\substack{\tilde{s}_1,\tilde{s}_2,a \\ \|\Omega(\tilde{s}_1)-\Omega(\tilde{s}_2)\|\leq \epsilon}} \left| u^\pi(\tilde{s}_1,a;g) - u^\pi(\tilde{s}_2,a;g) \right|.
\end{equation}
A large value of $\Delta^\pi_\epsilon(g)$ means that nearby observed states can have very different probabilities of successful transport. 
In manipulation, such gaps arise naturally near contact boundaries. 
A stable grasp and an unstable grasp may be close in object pose, gripper pose, and goal distance, but differ sharply in whether the cube remains controllable under future actions.

When we want to avoid off-support or physically irrelevant pairs, we use a support-restricted version. 
For a contact-relevant state-action support set $\mathcal C_g\subseteq \widetilde{\mathcal S}\times\mathcal A$, define:
\begin{equation}
\Delta^\pi_{\epsilon,\mathcal C}(g) = \sup_{\substack{(\tilde{s}_1,a),(\tilde{s}_2,a)\in\mathcal C_g \\ \|\Omega(\tilde{s}_1)-\Omega(\tilde{s}_2)\|\leq \epsilon}} \left| u^\pi(\tilde{s}_1,a;g) - u^\pi(\tilde{s}_2,a;g) \right|.
\end{equation}
This quantity formalizes the central difficulty: the observation metric may treat two states as neighbors even when their long-horizon transport futures are separated by interaction mode.

\begin{proposition}[Observation-smooth approximators incur error at interaction gaps]
Let $h_\theta(y,a;g)$ be an observation-based approximation to $u^\pi(\tilde{s},a;g)$. 
Assume that $h_\theta$ is $L$-Lipschitz in $y$ for fixed $a$ and $g$: $|h_\theta(y_1,a;g)-h_\theta(y_2,a;g)| \leq L\|y_1-y_2\|$. 
If there exist $\tilde{s}_1,\tilde{s}_2$ and $a$ such that $\|\Omega(\tilde{s}_1)-\Omega(\tilde{s}_2)\|\leq \epsilon$ and $|u^\pi(\tilde{s}_1,a;g) - u^\pi(\tilde{s}_2,a;g)| = \Delta$, then:
\begin{equation}
\max_{i\in\{1,2\}} \left| h_\theta(\Omega(\tilde{s}_i),a;g) - u^\pi(\tilde{s}_i,a;g) \right| \geq \frac{(\Delta-L\epsilon)_+}{2},
\end{equation}
where $(x)_+=\max\{x,0\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $y_i=\Omega(\tilde{s}_i)$, $u_i=u^\pi(\tilde{s}_i,a;g)$, and $\hat u_i=h_\theta(y_i,a;g)$. 
By the triangle inequality, $\Delta = |u_1-u_2| \leq |u_1-\hat u_1| + |\hat u_1-\hat u_2| + |\hat u_2-u_2|$. 
Since $h_\theta$ is $L$-Lipschitz in $y$, $|\hat u_1-\hat u_2| \leq L\|y_1-y_2\| \leq L\epsilon$. 
Therefore, $|u_1-\hat u_1| + |u_2-\hat u_2| \geq \Delta-L\epsilon$. 
Because the left-hand side is nonnegative, it is at least $(\Delta-L\epsilon)_+$. 
Hence at least one of the two errors must be at least $\frac{(\Delta-L\epsilon)_+}{2}$.
\end{proof}

The proposition shows that if the true reach-before-drop successor quantity changes sharply inside a small observation ball, then any observation-smooth approximation must make nontrivial error on at least one of the two states. 
The result is not a claim that exact successor measures cannot represent manipulation. 
The exact full-state successor measure can represent each interaction mode. 
The issue is approximation after observation compression: stable and unstable contact states may be aliased or placed close together in the observation metric, even though their future controllability differs.

A useful limiting case is exact observation aliasing. 
If $\Omega(\tilde{s}_1)=\Omega(\tilde{s}_2)$ but $|u^\pi(\tilde{s}_1,a;g) - u^\pi(\tilde{s}_2,a;g)| = \Delta$, then any observation-only approximator must incur error at least $\Delta/2$ on one of the two states, regardless of smoothness.

\subsection{Implication for low-rank inner-product successor models}

Many successor-measure methods use an inner-product approximation. 
In FB-style methods, for example, the successor measure is approximated by a low-rank product $M^{\pi_z}(s,a,s^+) \approx F_1(s,a,z)^\top B(s^+)$. 
After projecting the successor measure onto a goal-dependent reach-avoid quantity, this induces approximations of the form $h_\theta(y,a;g) = f_\theta(y,a)^\top b_\theta(g)$.

The previous proposition applies to this class whenever the induced scalar approximation is Lipschitz in $y$. 
A sufficient condition is bounded goal embedding norm and a Lipschitz state-action embedding. 
If $\|b_\theta(g)\|_2\leq B$ and $\|f_\theta(y_1,a)-f_\theta(y_2,a)\|_2 \leq L_f\|y_1-y_2\|$, then:
\begin{equation}
|h_\theta(y_1,a;g)-h_\theta(y_2,a;g)| = \left| \left(f_\theta(y_1,a)-f_\theta(y_2,a)\right)^\top b_\theta(g) \right| \leq BL_f\|y_1-y_2\|.
\end{equation}
Thus $h_\theta$ is $BL_f$-Lipschitz in $y$, and the lower bound becomes $\max_{i\in\{1,2\}} |h_\theta(\Omega(\tilde{s}_i),a;g) - u^\pi(\tilde{s}_i,a;g)| \geq \frac{(\Delta-BL_f\epsilon)_+}{2}$.

This does not mean that low-rank inner-product representations are intrinsically incapable of manipulation. 
Rather, it identifies a mismatch between their inductive bias and contact-rich dynamics. 
A single global inner-product geometry must simultaneously represent smooth geometric proximity and sharp interaction-dependent controllability. 
If it smooths across stable and unstable contact states, it must either underestimate stable transport states or overestimate nearby unstable-contact states.
