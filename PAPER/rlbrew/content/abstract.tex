\begin{abstract}
Many unsupervised reinforcement learning methods can be viewed as tractable approximations to policy-dependent successor measures.
We study why these methods perform unevenly in the state-based \ogbench{} \task{} environment.
Our hypothesis is that learned representations or scalar values may fail to distinguish nearby grasp states with different future occupancies.
We decompose rollouts into approach, control acquisition, maintain control, and transport across five methods: \gciql{}, \crl{}, \fb{}, \rldp{}, and
\psm{}.
All methods almost always complete approach and most acquire control, but the lower-performing methods fail mainly during maintain control and transport.
State-support gaps broadly covary with performance: \gciql{} shows almost no gap between successful and failed rollout states, whereas, for every
lower-performing method, failed pickup and carry states are farther from the offline data.
However, when initialized from in-hand states sampled directly from the offline dataset, the lower-performing methods still weakly rank grasp states
by eventual task success, while \gciql{} reaches a rank-AUC of $0.89$.
Linear probes further suggest that \fb{} contains task-success information that is not fully preserved by its scalar readout, whereas \rldp{} contains
less linearly accessible information.
Together, these findings show that effective successor-measure approximations for manipulation must not only reach and lift the object, but also assign
meaningfully different values to acquired-control states that support stable transport and those that lead to loss of control.
\end{abstract}
