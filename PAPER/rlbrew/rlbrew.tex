\begin{abstract}
Unsupervised reinforcement learning promises to learn reusable structure from reward-free data and later infer policies for downstream tasks. This promise is especially attractive for robotics, where reward design and online exploration are expensive. Yet manipulation exposes a failure mode that aggregate success rates can obscure: reaching an object, grasping it, maintaining contact, and transporting it to a goal are not interchangeable forms of goal reaching. They are distinct control regimes separated by contact-dependent bottlenecks. We study this issue on state observations in \ogbench{} \task{}, using phase-level diagnostics for three representative URL families: forward--backward representations (\fb{}), goal-conditioned implicit Q-learning (\gciql{}), and contrastive RL with a flow-matching behaviour-cloning actor (\crl{}+FlowBC). We extend the same state-only protocol toward proto-successor measures (\psm{}) and reward-free dynamics models. Our main finding is that \fb{} does not primarily fail by missing the cube. Across 350 state rollouts, it reaches the cube in all episodes and fails to grasp in only $7.7\%$ of episodes, but loses most of its gap to \gciql{} after pick-up: premature drops account for a $14.5$ percentage-point deficit and incorrect transport accounts for another $8.2$ points. Across the combined 850 state rollouts for \fb{} and \gciql{}, neither method terminates while holding the cube within one success radius of the goal. We interpret these results through the recent unifying view that URL methods approximate policy-dependent future reachability through successor measures and state equivalences. The key insight is that manipulation requires phase-conditioned controllability: two states that look close under object-goal geometry can have very different successor measures if they differ in contact phase, grasp stability, or controllability of the object. This suggests that progress on unsupervised RL for manipulation requires diagnostics and objectives that explicitly test bottleneck crossing, contact maintenance, and transport-phase successor consistency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Unsupervised reinforcement learning (URL) is motivated by a simple abstraction: learn a reusable description of the environment without task rewards, then reuse that description to solve many downstream tasks. 
This framing connects several algorithmic families, including successor representations \citep{dayan1993}, successor features \citep{barreto2017}, forward--backward representations \citep{touati2021,touati2023}, goal-conditioned and contrastive RL \citep{eysenbach2021clearning,eysenbach2022contrastive,ma2022offlinegcrl,wang2023quasimetric,park2024hilp}, mutual-information skill learning \citep{eysenbach2022infogeo}, proto-successor measures \citep{agarwal2025psm}, proto-value functions \citep{mahadevan2005,mahadevan2007,farebrother2023}, and controllable representations \citep{efroni2022,lamb2022,levine2024,rudolph2024}. A recent unification argues that these methods can be understood as different tractable approximations to successor measures, with state equivalences used to compress future reachability \citep{agarwal2025unified}. This perspective also connects back to older work on exploiting factored structure in MDPs, where the central question is which variables must be preserved for efficient planning and policy construction \citep{boutilier1995,guestrin2003,kearns1999}.

This paper asks where that abstraction breaks in manipulation. Cube manipulation is not merely navigation in a larger state space. Moving a gripper to a cube, closing around it, lifting it, maintaining contact, and transporting it to a goal are different regimes of control. The transition between regimes is mediated by contact. Before grasp, gripper motion and object motion are weakly coupled; after grasp, they are tightly coupled; after a drop, the system returns to a different controllability regime. A representation that captures coarse reachability can therefore be inadequate if it fails to preserve which phase the system is in and which actions maintain control of the object.

We deliberately focus on state observations. This removes visual encoder capacity as the central confound and lets us ask a sharper algorithmic question: even when object coordinates, gripper state, and proprioceptive features are available, do unsupervised RL methods learn representations that support the contact-dependent phases of manipulation? We use \ogbench{} \task{} as the evaluation domain because it is part of a benchmark designed for offline goal-conditioned RL and contains manipulation tasks that probe long-horizon composition and goal reaching \citep{park2025ogbench}.

Our central hypothesis is the following.

\begin{hypothesis}[Phase-bottleneck hypothesis]
Manipulation is hard for unsupervised RL because the downstream task is not a single smooth reachability problem. It is a multi-phase control problem in which contact states act as bottlenecks. URL methods that learn phase-blind state equivalences, goal-conditioned distances, or low-rank successor approximations may represent coarse reachability while failing to preserve the phase-specific controllability needed for grasp maintenance and object transport.
\end{hypothesis}

We make three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{A theory-level diagnosis.} We formulate manipulation as a phase-conditioned successor-measure problem and show why phase-blind abstractions can be successor-inconsistent around contact bottlenecks.
    \item \textbf{A state-only empirical protocol.} We decompose every \task{} rollout into approach, grasp, maintain-grasp, and transport outcomes. This turns aggregate success into a mechanistic failure profile.
    \item \textbf{A cross-method workshop agenda.} We report detailed state diagnostics for \fb{} and \gciql{}, apply the same outcome decomposition to \crl{}+FlowBC, and propose \psm{} and \dreamer{} diagnostics for basis coverage and contact-transition prediction.
\end{enumerate}

The state results support the bottleneck view. \fb{} reaches the cube in all analyzed rollouts and fails to grasp less often than \gciql{}, but loses heavily after pick-up. A pre-grasp intervention does not rescue performance. This suggests that the main issue is not simply entering the object-interaction regime; it is preserving control of the object and steering the coupled gripper-object system toward the target.

\section{Successor Measures and Phase-Conditioned Controllability}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{The common object behind many URL methods}

Consider a reward-free Markov decision process $(\Sspace,\Aspace,\Ptrans,\gamma)$. For a policy $\pi$, the successor measure from state-action pair $(s,a)$ to a measurable set $X\subseteq\Sspace$ is
\begin{equation}
    M^{\pi}(s,a,X)
    = \E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^t\ind\{s_{t+1}\in X\}\mid s_0=s,a_0=a\right].
\end{equation}
For a state reward $r$, policy evaluation can be written as a linear functional of the successor measure:
\begin{equation}
    Q^{\pi}_r(s,a)
    = \int_{\Sspace} M^{\pi}(s,a,ds^+) r(s^+).
\end{equation}
This identity explains why successor measures are a useful unifying object for URL: once future occupancy is known, downstream reward inference becomes a problem of assigning high successor mass to high-reward states \citep{touati2021,touati2023,agarwal2025unified}.

The exact object is intractable because it requires representing future occupancy for a large policy class. Practical methods therefore restrict the policy or representation class. \fb{} uses a low-rank factorization,
\begin{equation}
    M^{\pi_z}(s,a,s^+) \approx F(s,a,z)^{\top}B(s^+),
\end{equation}
where $z$ indexes a reward or policy embedding \citep{touati2021,touati2023}. Contrastive RL learns state-goal embeddings that rank reachable future states \citep{eysenbach2022contrastive}. GCRL learns values or policies conditioned on goals \citep{eysenbach2021clearning,ma2022offlinegcrl}. \psm{} decomposes successor measures into a proto-basis and uses the resulting basis for downstream inference \citep{agarwal2025psm}. Controllable-representation methods use inverse or multi-step inverse dynamics objectives to learn state abstractions that preserve action-relevant future predictability \citep{efroni2022,lamb2022,levine2024,rudolph2024}.

The common structure is compression. The unified URL view argues that these methods implicitly or explicitly define state equivalences: two states are treated as close when the representation predicts similar future reachability or control consequences \citep{agarwal2025unified}. In manipulation, this is exactly where the risk arises. States can be geometrically close while being dynamically inequivalent.

\subsection{Why manipulation breaks phase-blind equivalence}

We model a manipulation state as
\begin{equation}
    s = (x,o,g,c),
\end{equation}
where $x$ is robot proprioception, $o$ is object pose, $g$ is goal pose, and $c$ is a latent contact phase. For cube manipulation, the relevant phases include free approach, grasped transport, and unstable or dropped contact. The transition kernel is phase dependent:
\begin{equation}
    P(s'\mid s,a)=P_c(x',o',c'\mid x,o,c,a).
\end{equation}
When the cube is not grasped, object motion is weakly coupled to the gripper except near contact. When the cube is grasped, object motion is coupled to gripper motion. When contact is lost, the controllability of the object changes abruptly. A phase-blind representation can therefore merge states that have similar object-goal coordinates but different probabilities of future successful transport.

\begin{definition}[Phase-consistent successor abstraction]
An abstraction $\phi:\Sspace\to\mathcal{Z}$ is phase-consistent for a policy class $\Pi$ if $\phi(s_1)=\phi(s_2)$ implies
\begin{equation}
    M^{\pi}(s_1,a,X)=M^{\pi}(s_2,a,X),
    \qquad \forall \pi\in\Pi,\; a\in\Aspace,\; X\subseteq\Sspace.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

This condition is stronger than preserving object-goal distance. It requires preserving whether the object is controllable, whether the grasp is stable, and whether future actions can keep the system on the transport manifold.

\begin{proposition}[Phase-blind abstractions fail at contact bottlenecks]
Consider a two-phase manipulation MDP with contact phase $c\in\{0,1\}$ and target set $G=\{s:c=1,\|o-g\|\leq\epsilon\}$. Suppose two states $s_1=(x_1,o,g,0)$ and $s_2=(x_2,o,g,0)$ have identical object and goal coordinates but differ in the probability of entering the grasped phase under some policy $\pi$:
\begin{equation}
    \Prob_{\pi}(\tau_{c=1}<\infty\mid s_1,a)
    \neq
    \Prob_{\pi}(\tau_{c=1}<\infty\mid s_2,a).
\end{equation}
Then any abstraction $\phi$ that ignores the variables responsible for this difference and maps $\phi(s_1)=\phi(s_2)$ is not successor-consistent for the target set $G$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Reaching $G$ requires first entering the grasped phase. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    M^{\pi}(s_i,a,G)
    = \E_{\pi}\left[\gamma^{\tau_{c=1}}
    M^{\pi}(s_{\tau_{c=1}},a_{\tau_{c=1}},G)
    \mathbf{1}\{\tau_{c=1}<\infty\}\mid s_i,a\right].
\end{equation}
If the probability or timing of entering $c=1$ differs between $s_1$ and $s_2$, the successor mass assigned to $G$ differs unless downstream transport exactly cancels the difference, which is non-generic. Thus $M^{\pi}(s_1,a,G)\neq M^{\pi}(s_2,a,G)$, contradicting successor consistency.
\end{proof}

The proposition gives a concrete interpretation of manipulation failures. A method can learn a representation that is useful for broad reachability and still fail because the representation does not preserve contact acquisition, contact stability, or transport controllability.

\subsection{Method-specific predictions}

The phase-bottleneck view predicts related but distinguishable failure modes across URL methods. \gciql{} and other goal-conditioned value methods can succeed when the dataset contains enough bottleneck-crossing trajectories and the value function assigns correct credit to actions that preserve contact \citep{ma2022offlinegcrl,kostrikov2022iql,park2025ogbench}. \crl{} can learn useful reachability geometry, but contrastive separability does not guarantee phase-correct control: a state may be close to a goal in representation space while being dynamically unable to reach it without first crossing a contact bottleneck \citep{eysenbach2022contrastive}. \fb{} can retarget rewards through a compact successor factorization, but a single low-rank embedding $z=B(g)$ may entangle approach, grasp, and transport modes \citep{touati2021,touati2023}. \psm{} is valuable as a basis-coverage diagnostic: if no proto-policy occupies the stable grasp-and-transport phase, downstream inference cannot synthesize reliable transport from the basis \citep{agarwal2025psm}. A \dreamer{}-style world model provides an orthogonal diagnostic: if imagined rollouts predict free-space motion but not lift, slip, or contact loss, model-based pretraining will fail at the same structural boundary \citep{hafner2020dreamer,hafner2023dreamerv3}.

\section{Empirical Investigation}
\label{sec:empirical}

\subsection{Environment and methods}

We study the state-observation version of \ogbench{} \task{} over five held-out evaluation tasks, denoted \textit{task1}--\textit{task5}. The primary comparison is between \fb{} and \gciql{}. \fb{} learns a forward--backward successor factorization with a behavior-regularized actor. \gciql{} is the goal-conditioned IQL-style baseline used in the \ogbench{} reference implementation. We report interquartile mean (IQM) success with stratified-bootstrap confidence intervals for aggregate performance, then diagnose failures using deterministic closed-loop rollouts.

\subsection{Phase decomposition protocol}

Each rollout is assigned to exactly one terminal outcome: success, or the earliest failed phase in the manipulation pipeline. The ordered phases are approach, grasp, maintain grasp, and transport.

\section{Extending the State-Only Protocol}
\label{sec:extensions}

The same state-only decomposition extends to additional URL methods without changing the outcome labels. This is the main advantage of the protocol: it is method-agnostic at evaluation time. We instantiate it for one additional family --- contrastive RL with a flow-matching behaviour-cloning actor (\crl{}+FlowBC) --- and outline what the diagnostic would test for \psm{} and \dreamer{}.

\paragraph{\crl{}+FlowBC.} We apply the same 4-bucket decomposition to 10 \crl{}+FlowBC seeds trained on \task{} state observations. Table~\ref{tab:cross_method_template} reports the result. Unlike \fb{}, which loses most of its gap to \gciql{} \emph{after} pick-up (maintain $+$ transport account for $-22.7\pp$ out of $-18.1\pp$, partly offset by \fb{} grasping more reliably), \crl{}'s failures are spread across every post-approach phase. The result is a third distinct failure profile alongside \fb{}'s (post-pick-up) and \gciql{}'s (broadly competent): under \crl{}+FlowBC the bottleneck is broader, beginning at grasp and persisting through transport.

The \crl{} result above motivates the deeper representation-level test we leave to follow-up work: whether contrastive state-goal embeddings mis-rank phase-relevant states. Future-random separation should be computed not only over all states, but separately for pre-grasp, lifted, dropped, and transport states. For \psm{}, the key test is basis coverage: whether any proto-policy or proto-basis assigns substantial successor mass to the stable grasp-and-transport phase. For \dreamer{}, the key test is phase-transition prediction: whether the learned model predicts contact acquisition, lift, slip, and drop transitions accurately enough to support imagined control.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the state results suggest}

The empirical pattern supports the phase-bottleneck hypothesis. In state observations, \fb{} does not primarily fail because it cannot locate the cube. It reaches the cube in all analyzed rollouts, and initial grasp failures are relatively rare. The main failures occur after physical coupling should have been established. The pre-grasp intervention further suggests that establishing contact is not sufficient; the learned policy must preserve contact and steer the coupled system toward the goal.

This suggests that manipulation requires more than a representation of where rewarding states are. It requires a representation of which phase the system is in, which actions preserve that phase, and how controllability changes once the object becomes physically coupled to the robot.

\subsection{Why this matters for URL evaluation}

A single success score can make two misleading claims. First, it can make a method look close to solving the problem when it repeatedly fails at the same bottleneck. Second, it can make two methods look similar when their failure modes differ. Phase-level decomposition exposes both issues.

The stronger workshop message is therefore not just that \fb{} underperforms \gciql{} on \task{}. The stronger message is that URL methods can approximate future reachability while still failing to preserve the phase-conditioned controllability needed for contact-rich manipulation.

\subsection{Design implications}

The diagnosis points toward several algorithmic directions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Phase-conditioned representations.} Learn embeddings or successor measures conditioned on inferred contact phase rather than a single global state-goal representation.
    \item \textbf{Bottleneck-aware objectives.} Upweight rare transitions that establish or preserve control, such as lift, stable grasp, and drop-avoidance events.
    \item \textbf{Option-structured URL.} Treat approach, grasp, stabilize, and transport as latent options whose successor measures compose across bottlenecks.
    \item \textbf{Dynamics-assisted diagnostics.} Use world models not only for control, but to identify which phase transitions are poorly predicted.
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual interventions.} Evaluate policies from pre-grasped, near-goal, and post-drop states to distinguish contact acquisition from transport control.
\end{enumerate}
