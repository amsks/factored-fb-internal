\section{Setting}

Offline goal-conditioned RL. Two critics trained with exactly the same objective
(GCIQL: expectile TD on sparse reward), same data, same actor:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{bilinear}: $Q(s,a,g) = \langle \varphi(s,a), \psi(g)\rangle / \sqrt d$ ---
a dot product of two small embeddings;
\item \textbf{monolithic}: one MLP on the concatenated $(s,a,g)$.
\end{itemize}
On OGBench cube the bilinear critic reaches $0.93$ where the MLP reaches $0.73$
(behavior cloning alone: $0.09$). Nowhere else, once the comparison is run at the same
data scale, does it separate from the MLP --- and on the repaired Metaworld benchmark it
loses outright. The question is why, and the answer turned out to be simple.

\emph{A note on what counts as evidence.} Every number in this document is from a run
trained on ${\sim}10^6$ transitions, OGBench scale. Earlier arms at $2.5\times10^4$ to
$10^5$ transitions have been withdrawn: condition 4 below shows the effect under study
does not exist at those budgets even on cube, so those arms could not have measured it.
The one exception is the size ladder itself, which is \emph{about} dataset size. All
supporting numbers are in \texttt{table.tex} (\texttt{tab:main}).

\section{Why bilinear succeeds}

The bilinear $Q$ is a dot product of two small embeddings --- one for (state, action),
one for the goal. That forces all goals to share the same few building blocks. So what
the critic learns about goal $A$ in some states automatically transfers to \emph{new}
states it never saw paired with $A$. A big MLP is only accurate near its training data
--- when the policy improves and visits new states, the MLP's ``which action is best''
ranking turns to garbage there; the bilinear one stays right.

\paragraph{Proof in one line.} No actor training at all --- just sample 8 actions from a
BC policy and pick the one with the highest bilinear $Q$ --- matches the fully trained
policy on cube ($0.92$ vs.\ $0.93$). The MLP critic needs 64 samples to do the same
($0.61$ at 8, $0.74$ at 64). So the whole 20-point gap lives in how well each critic
\emph{ranks actions}, not in anything about actor training. (Two sanity checks: CRL,
which has the same dot-product architecture but is trained to predict where the
\emph{data-collecting} policy goes rather than what is \emph{best}, ranks actions at
chance level --- the architecture alone does nothing. And the same ranking test run on
each critic's value \emph{accuracy} instead of its ranking goes the other way --- the MLP
is the more accurate value estimator and still loses. Ranking is what matters.)

\section{When bilinear succeeds --- four plain conditions}\label{sec:when}

\paragraph{1. The task must finish within the distance range the discount can ``see''
(roughly tens to a couple hundred steps at $\gamma=0.99$).}
Cube: $\sim$40 steps $\to$ wins ($0.93$ vs.\ $0.73$).
Two-cube task: $\sim$400 steps $\to$ everything far away looks equally hopeless, there is
nothing to learn from $\to$ loses ($0.26$ at $d{=}50$, bimodal collapse at $d{=}512$,
vs.\ MLP $0.38$). The discount sweep makes the contrast monotone and extreme: as $\gamma$
goes $0.99 \to 0.995 \to 0.999$ the MLP climbs $0.38 \to 0.56 \to 0.63$ --- its best
two-cube numbers, confirming the task really was horizon-limited --- while bilinear falls
$0.26 \to 0.11 \to$ exactly $0.000$ on all three seeds. The longer the sight the task
needs, the better the MLP does and the harder bilinear dies: the dot product of two
normalized embeddings simply cannot output numbers as large as the values a longer
horizon requires (a one-scalar learnable output scale is the candidate fix, \S5).
The short end of the same prediction --- tasks finished in ${\sim}10$ steps, where
everything looks equally easy and there should likewise be no edge --- cannot be tested
on ManiSkill. It was rerun at cube-matched size ($10^6$ transitions) and cube-matched
structure, to close the loophole that its flatness might be the goal-input problem in
disguise, and it turns out to answer neither way, for two different reasons. With the baked-in goal
coordinates masked out, \texttt{ms-pick-cube} defeats \emph{every} method at
$0.02$--$0.04$ --- BC, MLP and bilinear alike --- and that floor is a contact-precision
limit rather than an evaluation artifact: re-evaluating the same checkpoints under the
corrected grasp-offset synthetic goal frame reproduces $0.02$--$0.04$, while the
goal-visible control re-evaluates at $0.98$. \texttt{ms-push-cube} is degenerate in the
opposite direction: its goal is determined by the cube's pose, so a goal-blind BC already
scores $0.99$ and guidance can only subtract from it. ManiSkill therefore supplies no
architecture-discriminating cell at $10^6$ in either direction (\texttt{tab:main}).

\paragraph{2. Every test goal must appear somewhere in the training data as a goal.}
The bilinear critic recombines what it has seen; it cannot invent values for never-seen
goals. We held out half the goal space during training and evaluated on all of it: the
bilinear critic lost to the MLP there ($0.36$ vs.\ $0.49$). (This is the opposite of what
the Bilinear Value Networks paper, ICLR 2022, reported for online RL with HER.)

\paragraph{3. The critic must be fed the true goal, and BC must not already solve the
task.}
Metaworld and ManiSkill bake the goal coordinates into the observation, and the standard
evaluation fed every critic a wrong, fixed goal --- so every goal-conditioned critic
looked useless there, and on easy data BC alone already scores $0.9{+}$ so no critic can
show anything. Repair all of it --- mask the baked-in goal coordinates out of
observations and goals, synthesize the eval goal per episode, drop the random-goal
relabeling, and run on $10^6$ Metaworld play transitions where the BC floor sits near
$0.5$ --- and the goal-conditioned critics finally work. But the winner is the MLP.
Repaired \texttt{mw-pick-place}: MLP $0.99$ against bilinear $0.92$, over a BC floor of
$0.44$. Repaired \texttt{mw-push}: MLP $0.73$ against bilinear $0.46$, over a BC floor of
$0.54$ --- bilinear is \emph{below the floor}, and bimodally so (per-seed $0.30/0.32/0.76$,
the same two-mode collapse signature as the two-cube task at $d{=}512$). Two more $10^6$
Metaworld regimes complete the sweep and both agree. On hot-noise data
(\texttt{play-noise-hard-1m}, no goal re-draws) the two critics tie
(push $0.53$ vs.\ $0.53$, pick-place $0.52$ vs.\ $0.45$ over BC floors of $0.33$--$0.34$).
On the hard resample regime ($\sigma{=}0.1$, goal-diverse relocation chaining, headroom
open on both sides --- the best remaining conditions for a separation) the MLP wins both
tasks outright: pick-place $0.97$ vs.\ $0.86$, push $0.81$ vs.\ $0.63$, with bilinear's
push seeds again widely spread ($0.48/0.66/0.74$). The message is sharper than ``the edge
is small on one-object tasks'': fixing the benchmark is what makes critics shine on
Metaworld, and across all six repaired cells it makes the bilinear one specifically
not-win (\texttt{tab:main}).

\paragraph{4. There must be enough data --- roughly $10^5$ transitions to see anything,
$2.5\times10^5$ for the full effect.}
The edge is not a property of the architecture alone; it has to be earned from samples.
Truncate the very dataset bilinear wins on --- whole trajectories dropped from
\texttt{cube-single} play --- and the gap appears gradually: at 48k transitions there is
\emph{no} gap ($0.34$ bilinear vs.\ $0.32$ MLP, inside one seed-sd), at 100k it is
half-formed ($0.70$ vs.\ $0.51$), and only by 250k is it at full size ($0.90$ vs.\ $0.70$),
after which it barely moves (1M: $0.93$ vs.\ $0.73$). BC stays flat at $0.05$--$0.09$
throughout, so this is a critic effect, not a general data effect
(\texttt{tab:main}; the truncation knob is a verified no-op at full size). This is why no
method comparison in this project is read off a dataset smaller than $10^6$: down in the
48k--100k band, ``there is no architectural edge on this benchmark'' and ``there is not
enough data here to form one'' produce the same table.

\section{Summary}

Bilinear wins when the task is \emph{reachable within the discount's sight}, the goals
are \emph{seen in training}, BC \emph{cannot} do it alone while the critic is fed the
\emph{true} goal, and there is \emph{enough data} for the shared structure to be
estimated at all --- because then its shared-building-block $Q$ keeps ranking actions
correctly in places the MLP has never been. Outside those four conditions --- very long
tasks, goals you never trained on, benchmarks whose observations leak the goal, or
datasets below ${\sim}10^5$ transitions --- the MLP with a well-chosen discount is the
safer choice. And ``the MLP'' is not the only alternative: on the repaired
\texttt{mw-push} cell that bilinear loses, the cell is held by neither of the two critics
this document contrasts but by a successor-measure critic at $0.82$ (which in turn fails
pick-place at $0.13$). No critic dominates everywhere; what this project adds is that you
can tell \emph{in advance} which one to use.

\section{A one-parameter fix for the long-horizon failure}

The long-horizon failure (condition 1) has a mechanical cause that suggests its own fix.
The two embeddings are normalized inside the network, so their dot product can only
output numbers up to about $\sqrt d$ in size --- around $75$ at $d{=}50$. But the values
a task demands grow with the horizon: at $\gamma=0.99$ they reach $-100$, at
$\gamma=0.995$ around $-200$, at $\gamma=0.999$ around $-1000$. The MLP just outputs
bigger numbers; the dot product physically cannot. Making $d$ bigger does raise the cap,
but only like $\sqrt d$ --- reaching $-1000$ from $75$ would take roughly $9000$
dimensions, and it wastes capacity, because $d$ then controls two unrelated things at
once: how much structure is shared across goals, and how big the outputs can get.

The fix is to separate the two with a single learnable scalar on the output:
\[
Q(s,a,g) \;=\; e^{s}\cdot \langle \varphi(s,a), \psi(g)\rangle/\sqrt d,
\qquad s \text{ learnable, initialized at } 0 .
\]
The exponent keeps the scale positive and lets it grow multiplicatively; at
initialization the critic is exactly the original one. Now the number size is free ---
like the MLP's output layer --- and $d$ is left to do the one job it is good at. If this
plus a longer-sighted discount recovers the bilinear critic on the two-cube task, it also
proves the failure there was number size, not the shared-structure idea; if it does not,
the failure is deeper.

Status: the prediction remains on record and \emph{untested}. The first attempt (six
runs: $\gamma\in\{0.995, 0.999\}$, 3 seeds, 2026-08-05) produced no data --- every run
crashed at initialization because the scale parameter was created outside a
\texttt{@compact} method (a Flax constraint; \texttt{impls/utils/networks.py}). What did
land meanwhile raises the stakes: without the scale, $\gamma{=}0.999$ lifts the MLP to
$0.63$ while bilinear collapses to $0.000$ on every seed, exactly the regime the
one-scalar fix is supposed to rescue. The runs need a code fix and a relaunch.
